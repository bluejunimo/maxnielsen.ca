What it is

We designed an interactive transit fare map that replaces the existing map (headliner) on TransLink’s SkyTrain (LRT) ticket machines.

Its goal is to reduce confusion about the zone-based fare system amongst new and inexperienced riders. Our map displays the zone system based on the time of day and the fare riders need to get to their destination and visualizes where riders can travel when selecting a specific ticket from the machine.

What I learned

Our team of four were all involved in learning and applying various design methods. Outside of developing hard skills, I focused on crafting presentations grounded in storytelling and hosting participatory design workshops with stakeholders.

Working on my hard skills

Read less

From the six methods we used, our early success of conducting ethnography and analyzing its qualitative data put us on a stable path to our final design concept.

Because of TransLink’s size, we focused our research at SkyTrain station entrances. We learned how SkyTrain entrances are a part of a riders transit journey, why riders used certain methods of payment over others, and how they used ticket machines.

We did fourteen interviews (which I interviewed four of them) and conducting thematic analysis outlined 27 different themes within the data. As you can expect, these were very specific patterns. I advocated for a few more rounds of thematic analysis by explaining how creating higher-level themes could help us keep an open perspective on potential solutions. I got buy-in from my classmates and we burned the midnight oil to end up with six insights after two more rounds of analysis.

Irina (one of my teammates) then suggested using a priority matrix to understand each insight’s level of impact on users and their probable importance to TransLink. We landed on three insights that we stuck with throughout the project.

Through our ethnographic process, we not only created rich insights with multiple rounds of analysis, but I also saw the value of what narrowing your design focus has on a project. Focusing on a small quantity of specific issues made the project less uncertain as to who we were designing for which helped us feel grounded in the design process and ultimately influenced our final concept.

Hosting stakeholder workshops

Read less

I’ve never been an interviewer or a workshop host before this semester. But this project taught me those skills and taught me it fast. It was great to learn how to connect with both transit riders and TransLink employees.

We used some brainstorming workshop material content from a previous workshop I hosted within our team. We talked about and used what we felt worked well in that session and some other activities from our class’s readings on participatory workshops. We then created questions and activities that align with specific goals we created. As we only had so much time with our participants (especially with TransLink employees), we narrowed down our questions and activities through deep discussion and priority matrices.

As with any first timer, there’s bound to be luck but also messiness. The first workshop was a resounding success overall. We gathered great insights and our participants were very comfortable with one another. However, one participant commented to me that I was giving them too many options to complete the tasks. It felt like to them that I should know “what happens and what is going to provide the most helpful responses for your project” (Personal communication, March 9, 2025). I reflected on that and made extra sure that our next workshops were more precisely planned out.

But some of that luck wore off in the second workshop. The participants weren’t as engaged and we missed the mark on making them comfortable. There was tension in the room that never went away. Part of that was probably on me for not stepping in and fixing the atmosphere, so that’s something I need to learn how to do.

Our final workshop with stakeholders was with TransLink. We spent extra time going over our workshop material to make sure we could fit it in under the 30 minutes of time we had. One last push the night before, I edited the questions to make sure that we weren’t repeating ourselves and condensing them to make sure we get the most value out of each question. That paid off and the employees were very engaged and involved throughout the meeting, chipping in to one another’s suggestions and providing great direction for us to move forward with. How did I do with hosting that one? Well it was stressful, but it was also worth it because they came to see the final presentation.

I think that the format and questions of the workshops gave us great insights into riders and TransLink’s design philosophy. The medium allowed us to connect on a greater level to the participants than a typical interview and gave us greater insights into what riders value about taking transit.

Bringing users’ voices into the business world

Read less

I’d be lying if I said that working with TransLink’s CX team wasn’t stress-inducing. Expectations of our results felt high: we were undergraduates that had never collaborated with professional designers before.

One part of a design ethnographer’s job is to “bring the voices and lives of the end user or consumer out of the home and into the business world” (Design Ethnography).

We needed to understand that buy-in is as important as the ethnography itself. Without it, projects get canned. For us, buy-in was making the collaboration worthwhile for TransLink. Some of it was done through sheer hard work, but the other half involved strategic presenting of our results.

I took multiple steps to create rich presentations for TransLink. The final presentation was the culmination of all of these efforts:

  • I trimmed off fat from our class presentation to align with our shared knowledge. For example, TransLink already knows how their ticket machines work.
  • We changed terminology to be more familiar to TransLink. For example, “headliner” is the official term used internally for the maps on ticket machines.
  • I used persona-driven stories in combination with clear imagery to give context to the issues we gathered with our ethnography.
  • I utilized Figma’s powerful prototyping tools to integrate our interactive mock-up into our slides to clearly articulate how our design works.

The combination of the points above led us to have a succinct, professional, and visually engaging final presentation based in ethnographic data.

Web interface features

I went on to implement the new voting system in our Arduino code and build the web interface.

The web interface’s original plan was to only show the state of the physical devices. This was pointless for everyone in the space when the devices already had state indicators. To incentivize visiting the website, I used Spotify, Tidal, and Deezer’s APIs to display song details. I also added Add to playlist links that allowed users to quickly add the song to their music streaming playlist. This gave non-voters a reason to interact with the system.

Reflection

As the project progressed, it became apparent that workloads were unbalanced. I took on too many tasks as my knowledge of web, interaction, and system design aligned with this course’s content. My team members also stuck to their strengths in physical prototyping. Next time, I will focus on developing new skills while acting as a mentor for others taking over my expected job. This way, we can distribute tasks more evenly and foster better learning outcomes for each team member.
